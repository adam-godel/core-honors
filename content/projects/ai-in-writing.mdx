The Core texts are the texts covered in Boston University's Core Curriculum sequence. These interesting texts can teach us a lot about the role of AI in writing. In this essay, I will discuss three of these texts: Michel de Montaigne's "On the Education of Children," Miguel de Cervantes' *Don Quixote*, and Immanuel Kant's *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*. I will argue that these texts can be closely connected to the role AI can play in our writing.

In "On the Education of Children," Michel de Montaigne discusses how students should not just be taught how to memorize, but how to actually critically think for themselves. Montaigne says that education should be conducted with a "firm gentleness," so that the student is oriented properly but is still pushing themselves to study what they want. This is very closely related to AI since AI takes the aspect of critical thinking away from the person using it, and just gives them boilerplate answers.

*Don Quixote*, on the other hand, expresses this connection more subtly through the character of Sansón Carrasco. Carrasco is a university student with a bachelor's degree, referring to himself by the title "Bachelor." In Part Two of the text, Carrasco notes that the interpolated stories in Part One that didn't focus on Don Quixote, the main character, were useless in the text. Here, Cervantes portrays Carrasco as doing a surface reading of Part One of the text, which can be tied to AI in the way that it can summarize texts from its training data, but not examine them closely or build connections on them in the way a human could.

Immanuel Kant's "categorical imperative," described in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, establishes the principle that people should act based on morals that can be applied as universal laws. When someone acts in a certain way, they should be able to justify everyone acting in that same way. This is closely related to AI since it essentially ingests information and mushes it together, producing a homogenous result that affects both the tone and content of its responses. By relying on AI, the voices of writers are homogenized, perhaps keeping the value in the meaning of the words but essentially suppressing the person who wrote it.

Well, wait, hold on a minute. Is that really true? One other thing I know about AI is that it can give widely different, even contradictory responses to the same prompt. That doesn't sound reminiscent of the categorical imperative at all. At the end of the day, an AI model is an algorithm taking in tons of training data and *randomly* extracting a result. There is inherently a degree of spontaneity and entropy in every AI models—if you think about it, if they didn't have that, they would just be a search engine over a huge dataset. Spontaneity is what makes them seem more "human."

Likewise, I need to think more closely about what exactly Kant is saying when he discusses the categorical imperative. Kant, a proponent of the Enlightenment, believed that every human has an equal capacity of reason, and that eventually society will progress past the practices of exploitation, inequality, and so on. I think Kant was trying to make an argument that if we truly consider humans to be rational agents, we need to be able to justify our own actions as the actions anyone would have taken, and we should have always taken the same action in different circumstances.

A major problem with AI is that it masquerades as a sort of "rational agent" while in reality really not being one. AI model responses will often heavily weight the prompt in their response to a question. If a prompt seems more inclined to believe one thing compared to a different prompt which seems more inclined to believe an opposing thing, the model will usually not respond with an answer directly contradictory to one of the prompts. This leads to the "hallucination" effect many AI models display. This is completely opposed to Kant's idea that we should obey the categorical imperative. Regardless of if you believe in the categorical imperative or its attainability, AI models are definitively *not* acting under this framework.

But do we as humans, either? I don't think so! Take the example above. I started by arguing that Kant's categorical imperative is closely related to the summarizing nature of AI. I then argued that that's actually not really true, since AI inherently has a strong element of spontaneity and aligns closely to the perspective of the prompt it's given. Both of these aren't necessarily false, but I align much more strongly with the latter view.

What this indicates, in my opinion, is that part of what makes AI so enticing is how close to "human" it really is in many ways. The ways the responses of AI models strike us as human is what makes them so compelling; their divergences, on the other hand, are what can make them feel so uncanny. But unlike an AI model, I truly learn from my inquisitions into a subject. I would need more information to change back my mind. An AI model could change back sporadically depending on the prompt. 

I truly believe that there is a value to the human thought process of things. So maybe a "truer" way to interrogate a fundamental question in writing—for the purpose of evaluation, at least—is to show your thought process on paper. This has two major advantages. First of all, it allows the evaluator (the reader, the professor, the grader, etc.) to see more closely how you got to your conclusions. Second of all, it makes the *voice* of the writer a lot stronger. It's easier to tell both who is writing and how they think because the text *sounds* more strongly like them.

I do think there is a place for more impersonal writing. In a journalistic context, for example, embedding the voice of the author might muddle the content. The readers typically want to see a set of content, the "facts," and don't really care who's writing it. There also might be a large group of writers that contribute. Impersonal writing is also heavily present in a lot of research work, where the author's experiences or framing matter less than the quantitative data collected. 

However, are undergraduate writing assignments the place for this type of impersonal writing? Maybe we should try to give more opportunities for personalized, stream-of-consciousness-style writing.
